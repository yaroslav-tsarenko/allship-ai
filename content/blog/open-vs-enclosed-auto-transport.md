Shipping a car comes down to one early decision: should it ride on an open trailer or inside an enclosed one? The answer depends less on how much you love your car and more on what could actually go wrong on the road, and how much it would cost to fix.

This guide compares the two options, lists the insurance questions worth asking, and covers how to get the car ready for pickup.

## The short version

| | Open transport | Enclosed transport |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure | Car is exposed to weather, dust and road debris | Car is covered on all sides |
| Capacity | Usually carries more vehicles per trip | Usually carries fewer vehicles per trip |
| Availability | Widely available on most routes | Fewer trucks, so scheduling can take longer |
| Loading | Ramps, often steep | Often liftgate or lower-angle ramps available |
| Price | Lower, because cost is shared across more cars | Higher, because fewer cars share the trip |
| Typical use | Daily drivers, used cars, dealer inventory, relocations | Classic, exotic, collector and show cars, high-value or low-clearance vehicles |

Exact prices vary with distance, route, season, vehicle size and whether the car runs. Our [car shipping cost calculator](/tools/car-shipping-cost-calculator) shows how those factors interact for your route.

## Open auto transport

Open carriers are the trailers you see on the highway with cars stacked on two levels. They are the standard way to move vehicles in the US, from dealer inventory to cross-country relocations.

### What you get

- **More options.** Because there are more open trucks on the road, it is usually easier to find one that matches your dates and route.
- **Lower cost.** An open trailer carries more cars per trip, so each customer pays a smaller share of fuel, driver time and tolls.
- **Faster scheduling.** More trucks means more chances that one is already passing near your pickup location.

### What you accept

Your car rides in the open air for the whole trip. That means rain, sun, dust, bugs and the chance of small chips from road debris. On a two-level trailer, a car on the lower deck can also be exposed to drips from vehicles above it.

For a typical daily driver, these risks are similar to what the car faces on a long road trip. For a car with a show-quality finish, they are not.

## Enclosed auto transport

Enclosed carriers use a trailer with solid walls and a roof. Some are single-car or two-car trailers pulled by a pickup. Others are full-size trailers that carry several vehicles.

### What you get

- **Protection from the elements.** No rain, sun, hail or road grime reaches the car.
- **Protection from debris.** Rocks and road spray stay outside.
- **Gentler loading on many trucks.** Many enclosed carriers use liftgates or low-angle ramps, which matters for cars with very low ground clearance or long front overhangs.
- **Privacy.** The car is not visible from the road, which some owners of rare vehicles prefer.

### What you accept

Enclosed transport costs more because fewer cars share each trip. There are also fewer enclosed trucks, so you may need more flexibility on pickup dates, especially on less-traveled routes.

## Which one is worth it?

Choose **open** if:

- [ ] The car is a daily driver, a used car, or a standard new car
- [ ] Small cosmetic marks would be an annoyance, not a financial loss
- [ ] You want the widest choice of dates and carriers
- [ ] Budget matters more than maximum protection

Choose **enclosed** if:

- [ ] The car is a classic, exotic, collector or show vehicle
- [ ] It has fresh paint, a restoration or a specialty finish
- [ ] It has very low ground clearance that makes steep ramps a problem
- [ ] It is not road-legal or has parts that should not be exposed to weather
- [ ] Replacing or repairing the finish would cost far more than the price difference

A useful test: compare the extra cost of enclosed transport with the cost of repairing a paint chip or a scratched panel on your specific car. If the repair is cheap, open is usually the better value. If it is expensive or the car's value depends on original condition, enclosed earns its price.

## Check the carrier before you book

Whichever trailer type you choose, check who is moving the car. FMCSA's [consumer advisory on auto transporters](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/resources/consumer-advisory-automobile-transporters) says both interstate auto carriers and auto transport brokers must be registered with FMCSA, and warns consumers to avoid any company that does not prominently display its MC number.

A few things to look for in the [SAFER Company Snapshot](https://safer.fmcsa.dot.gov/CompanySnapshot.aspx):

- Entity type: carrier, broker or both
- USDOT status: active
- Operating authority: authorized for property, for a carrier hauling vehicles for hire across state lines
- Out-of-service orders: none

If you book through a broker, ask which carrier will actually pick up your car and check that carrier too. FMCSA's [types of operating authority](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/types-operating-authority) page explains that a broker arranges transportation but does not take possession of the property.

FMCSA also suggests paying by credit or debit card and monitoring the charges so you are billed only what you agreed to.

## Insurance questions to ask

This is where many car owners get caught out. FMCSA's [insurance filing requirements](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/insurance-filing-requirements) list the public liability coverage that property carriers must file. Cargo insurance is listed as a filing requirement for household goods carriers, so coverage for the car you are shipping depends on the auto carrier's own policy.

Ask every carrier these questions and get the answers in writing:

1. **Can you send a current certificate of insurance?** Check that the insured name matches the company on the FMCSA record and that the policy dates are current.
2. **Do you carry cargo coverage, and what is the limit per vehicle?** Make sure the limit is at least the value of your car.
3. **What is the deductible, and who pays it?** Some carriers pass the deductible to the customer on smaller claims.
4. **What is excluded?** Common questions include weather damage, damage on open trailers from road debris, mechanical issues and pre-existing damage.
5. **Are personal items in the car covered?** Usually they are not.
6. **How do I report damage, and how quickly?** Know the process before delivery day.

Also call your own auto insurer. Some personal auto policies extend coverage while the car is being transported, and knowing your position helps if there is a claim.

## How to prep your car for pickup

Good preparation makes the inspection faster and protects you if there is a dispute later.

### Before pickup

- [ ] Wash the car so existing scratches and dents are visible
- [ ] Take dated photos and a walkaround video of every panel, the wheels, the roof and the interior
- [ ] Note existing damage and mileage
- [ ] Remove personal items, loose accessories, toll tags and parking passes
- [ ] Remove or secure aftermarket parts such as spoilers, roof racks and antennas
- [ ] Leave a modest amount of fuel, as the carrier advises
- [ ] Check for fluid leaks and tell the carrier about any you find
- [ ] Make sure the battery is charged and the tires are inflated
- [ ] Disable the alarm, or tell the driver how to turn it off
- [ ] Hand over one key, not your only key

### At pickup

The driver should inspect the car with you and record its condition on the bill of lading or inspection report. Compare it with your own photos. Do not sign a report that leaves out damage you can see, or that marks damage that is not there.

### At delivery

Inspect the car before you sign, ideally in daylight. Walk around it with the same care you used at pickup and compare it against your photos. Note any new damage on the delivery paperwork, then take photos before the driver leaves.

## Bottom line

Open transport is the sensible default for most cars: more trucks, more dates and a lower price. Enclosed transport is worth it when exposure could cause expensive or irreversible damage, such as on a classic, exotic or freshly painted car. Either way, verify the carrier with FMCSA, get the cargo insurance details in writing, and document the car's condition at both ends.
