Moving fraud rarely looks like fraud at the start. It looks like a friendly salesperson, a quote well below everyone else's, and a request for a deposit to "lock in the date." The trouble shows up on moving day or, worse, after your belongings are already on the truck.

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) runs a consumer program called Protect Your Move that documents the patterns. This guide pulls together its red flags, explains the paperwork you are entitled to, and shows how holding payment in escrow changes the balance of power.

## How a typical moving scam works

Most moving scams follow one of a few scripts:

- **The lowball quote.** You get a price over the phone or online without anyone seeing your home. Once your things are loaded, the "real" price appears.
- **The hostage load.** The mover refuses to deliver until you pay much more than agreed.
- **The deposit and disappear.** You pay a large deposit, and the company stops answering.
- **The broker shell.** A company that sounds like a mover is actually an unregistered middleman who sells your job to whoever will take it.

Each one depends on the same thing: getting your money, or your belongings, before you can check who you are dealing with.

## FMCSA's red flags

FMCSA publishes a list of [red flags of moving fraud](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/red-flags). In plain terms, be cautious if the mover or broker:

1. Gives an estimate over the phone or online, sight unseen, without inspecting your belongings
2. Will not give you a written estimate, or says the price will be set after loading
3. Demands cash or a large deposit before the move
4. Asks you to sign blank documents
5. Does not give you the *Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move* booklet and the *Ready to Move* brochure
6. Has a website with no local address and no information about registration or insurance
7. Claims all of your goods are covered by its insurance
8. Answers the phone with a generic "Movers" or "Moving company" instead of a business name
9. Has offices or a warehouse that are in poor condition or do not exist
10. Shows up on moving day with a rental truck instead of a company-owned or marked truck
11. Claims you have more belongings than estimated

Any one of these deserves a question. Two or three together should end the conversation.

## The red flags in more detail

### Large deposits and cash

A mover that wants a big payment up front has less reason to show up, and you have less leverage if they do not. If you must pay something before the move, use a credit card so you can dispute the charge. Avoid cash, wire transfers, payment apps and gift cards.

### No on-site estimate

Federal rules require interstate movers to do a physical survey of your household goods before giving a written estimate. You can waive the survey, but only in writing, signed before the shipment is loaded. A mover who skips the survey and quotes from a short phone call has nothing tying the price to your actual inventory, which is how "you have more stuff than we thought" happens on moving day.

### Binding vs. non-binding estimates

Before you sign the bill of lading, the mover has to give you a written estimate and say whether it is binding or non-binding. FMCSA's [steps to select a mover](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/select-mover) point to the 110 percent rule: on a non-binding estimate, the mover cannot require you to pay more than 110 percent of the estimate at delivery. Extra charges beyond that are billed afterward, not used as a condition for unloading.

### Unregistered movers and brokers

Interstate movers must have a USDOT number, and they need operating authority to carry household goods. Brokers must be registered as brokers and must work only with registered movers. Check the company in FMCSA's [mover search tool](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/search-mover) before you sign anything. Our guide to verifying USDOT and MC numbers walks through the record field by field.

### Missing paperwork

Interstate movers must give you the federal booklet [*Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move*](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/sites/fmcsa.dot.gov/files/2023-10/FMCSA_R&R_Handbook_Web_v1.pdf). It is worth reading before you book, not after. Among other things, it explains:

- The difference between binding and non-binding estimates
- Full Value Protection, which is the default level of mover liability unless you waive it in writing
- What the bill of lading and inventory should include
- That you have 9 months from delivery to file a claim, and that the mover then has 30 days to acknowledge it and 120 days to respond

A mover who does not hand you this booklet is either careless or counting on you not knowing your rights.

## A pre-booking checklist

- [ ] Company's legal name, USDOT number and MC number in writing
- [ ] FMCSA record shows active registration and authority for household goods
- [ ] Insurance on file with FMCSA
- [ ] Written estimate based on an inspection of your belongings, marked binding or non-binding
- [ ] *Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move* and *Ready to Move* received
- [ ] Clear answer on who will do the move, if you booked through a broker
- [ ] Valuation option chosen and written on the paperwork
- [ ] No blank fields on anything you sign
- [ ] Payment method that does not require a large up-front cash or wire payment

## How escrow payment protects you

The scams above all work because money changes hands before the job is done. Escrow flips that.

### What escrow means in general

In an escrow arrangement, a neutral party holds the payment while the job is in progress. The buyer pays in full up front, so the seller knows the money exists. But the seller does not receive it until an agreed condition is met. For a move, that condition is delivery.

This protects both sides:

- **You** do not hand cash to a mover who then raises the price or vanishes. The money sits with a third party.
- **The mover** knows the funds are real and will be released when the work is done, so there is no reason to hold your goods for payment.

### What escrow does not replace

Escrow controls when the carrier is paid. It does not replace the mover's liability for loss or damage, and it does not replace your right to file a claim under federal rules. Read your estimate, choose a valuation level, and keep your inventory and bill of lading.

## If you think you have been scammed

FMCSA says it cannot resolve individual claims against a moving company, but it does want to hear about fraud. You can [file a complaint](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/file-a-complaint) online through the National Consumer Complaint Database or by calling 888-368-7238. Have your estimate, bill of lading, inventory and the company's USDOT or MC number ready.

If you paid by credit card, contact your card issuer as well. For local moves within one state, your state attorney general or consumer protection office is often the right place to start.

## Bottom line

Moving scams depend on getting paid before you can push back. Insist on a registered mover, a written estimate based on an inspection, the federal booklet, and a payment setup where the mover is paid after delivery.
