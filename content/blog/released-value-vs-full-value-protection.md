Every interstate household goods move comes with a choice that many people sign through without reading: how much the mover is liable for if something is lost or damaged. Federal rules give you two levels, and the difference between them is enormous.

This guide explains released value and full value protection, what each one pays, and how to decide.

## Valuation is not insurance

The first thing to understand is the vocabulary. When movers talk about "valuation," they mean the mover's legal liability for your belongings. It is set by federal rules, not by an insurance policy.

FMCSA's brochure on [valuation and insurance options](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/sites/fmcsa.dot.gov/files/2023-07/PYM_Understanding%20Valuation_March23%20%28002%29.pdf) separates the two clearly. Valuation is what the mover owes you. Insurance is something you can buy separately from a third party, and its cost is not part of the basic moving charges.

That distinction matters because one of FMCSA's red flags for moving fraud is a mover claiming that "all goods are covered by their insurance." The mover's insurance filings with FMCSA exist to meet federal minimums. They are not a promise to replace your couch.

## The two federal liability levels

For interstate moves, the rules come from 49 CFR 375.201 and the Surface Transportation Board's released rates order.

| | Full Value Protection | Released Value |
|---|---|---|
| Default? | Yes, unless you waive it in writing | Only if you sign a waiver choosing it |
| Mover's liability | Replacement value of lost or damaged goods | 60 cents per pound per article |
| Cost to you | Varies by mover, may depend on the deductible you choose | No extra charge |
| How damage is handled | Mover repairs, replaces, or pays a cash settlement | Payment based on the item's weight |
| High-value items | Must be listed on shipping documents if worth more than $100 per pound | Weight-based limit applies regardless of value |

## Released value: 60 cents per pound per article

Released value is the minimal level of protection. The Surface Transportation Board says on its [lost or damaged items page](https://www.stb.gov/resources/need-assistance/hhg-moving/hhg-lost-or-damaged-items/) that the limited liability level "is currently set at $0.60 per pound for each item." FMCSA uses the same figure.

The math is simple, and that is the problem. Compensation is based on weight, not value:

- FMCSA's valuation brochure uses the example of a 10-pound stereo worth $1,000. Under released value, the mover owes $6.
- FMCSA's [loss and damage advisory](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/resources/consumer-advisory-loss-and-damage) gives another example: a $1,500 TV weighing 51 pounds would be covered for $30.60.

Released value costs nothing extra, which is why it can look attractive on a quote. But to get it, you must sign a specific statement on the bill of lading or contract agreeing to it. FMCSA's advisory is blunt: "60 cents a pound is not adequate coverage."

Released value can make sense if your shipment is mostly heavy, low-value items, or if you already have separate coverage for your belongings in transit. For most households, it leaves a large gap.

## Full value protection: the default

Under full value protection, the mover is liable for the replacement value of goods that are lost, damaged, destroyed or not delivered. FMCSA's [liability and protection page](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/consumer-protection/protect-your-move/are-you-moving/liability-protection) says that for each item, the mover can choose to:

- Repair the item
- Replace it with a similar item
- Pay a cash settlement for the cost of repair or the current market replacement value

The federal *Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move* booklet says the initial estimate you receive must include full value protection, and your shipment moves at that level unless you waive it.

### What it costs

FMCSA says the cost of full value protection varies by mover and may be subject to different deductible levels that can reduce your cost. A higher deductible means you absorb more of a claim yourself in exchange for a lower valuation charge. Ask each mover to show you the valuation charge and deductible options in writing so you can compare them on the same basis.

### Items of extraordinary value

There is an important limit even under full value protection. FMCSA defines an article of high or extraordinary value as any item worth more than $100 per pound. Jewelry, silverware, china, furs and antiques are typical examples.

If those items are not specifically listed on your shipping documents, the mover may limit its liability for them. Ask for a written explanation of how the mover handles these items before your move.

FMCSA's advisory goes further and suggests keeping small, high-value items such as jewelry or coin collections out of the household goods shipment entirely. If you cannot carry them yourself, consider shipping them separately where they can be fully insured.

## Third-party insurance

If you want more protection, or want to cover things the mover's liability does not, you can buy insurance from a third party. FMCSA recommends checking your homeowner's insurance first, because some policies already provide coverage for goods in transit or offer it as an add-on.

When comparing options, ask:

- [ ] Does my homeowner's or renter's policy cover belongings during an interstate move?
- [ ] Is coverage based on replacement cost or actual cash value?
- [ ] Are high-value items covered, and do they need to be scheduled?
- [ ] What is the deductible?
- [ ] Does the policy exclude items I packed myself?

## How claims work

Whatever level you choose, the claims timeline is set by federal rules and explained in the *Your Rights and Responsibilities* booklet:

- You have **9 months** from delivery to file a written claim. If the whole shipment is lost, the clock starts from the date it should have been delivered.
- The mover has **30 days** to acknowledge your claim.
- The mover then has **120 days** to give you a disposition.

If you and the mover cannot agree, interstate movers must offer a dispute settlement program. Under [49 CFR 375.211](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-375/subpart-B/section-375.211), arbitration must be binding for claims of $10,000 or less if you request it.

### Protect your claim on moving day

- Keep a copy of the inventory and bill of lading
- Photograph valuable items before packing, and record serial numbers on electronics
- Check items as they come off the truck and note visible damage on the delivery paperwork before signing
- Keep damaged items and packaging until the claim is settled

## How to choose

Ask yourself one question: if the truck rolled over, how much would it cost to replace everything on it?

If the answer is more than a few thousand dollars, released value will not come close. Full value protection, possibly combined with a sensible deductible, is the safer choice for most households. Released value fits only when the shipment is low value, or you have confirmed separate coverage.

## Bottom line

Full value protection is the default for interstate moves, and released value only applies if you waive it in writing. At 60 cents per pound per article, released value pays very little for most household items. Choose full value protection unless you have a clear reason not to, list anything worth more than $100 per pound, and keep your paperwork until every claim is closed.
