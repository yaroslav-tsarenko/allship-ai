If you ship less-than-truckload (LTL) freight, the number on your bill of lading that matters most after weight is the freight class. Get it wrong and the carrier can reclassify the shipment after pickup, which usually means a higher invoice and a reclassification fee.

In July 2025, the industry's classification system changed in a big way. This guide explains how freight class works now, how to measure and calculate density, and why it affects your price.

## What freight class is

Freight class comes from the National Motor Freight Classification (NMFC), published by the National Motor Freight Traffic Association (NMFTA). According to [NMFTA](https://nmfta.org/standards/classification/), products are assigned classes from 50 to 500 based on four transportation characteristics:

| Characteristic | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Density | How much the freight weighs relative to the space it takes up |
| Handling | How easy it is to move across docks and into and out of trailers |
| Stowability | How efficiently it can be loaded alongside other freight |
| Liability | How likely it is to be damaged, or to damage other freight |

The basic logic is straightforward. Lower class numbers go to freight that is dense and easy to handle and stow. Higher class numbers go to freight that is light for its size, awkward, fragile or hazardous.

## Why class affects your LTL price

LTL carriers combine shipments from many customers in one trailer. Each shipment competes for the same limited floor space and weight capacity. A pallet of light, bulky goods takes up as much room as a pallet of dense goods but pays for less weight, so carriers charge more per pound for it.

NMFTA puts it simply: "The denser and easier to move, the lower the class—and the lower the cost. Bulkier, fragile, or hazardous items have higher classes." Its guidance on [packaging and class](https://nmfta.org/news/packaging-and-class-how-packaging-decisions-change-density-and-your-invoice/) adds that larger cubes generally result in lower densities, and therefore higher classes and costs.

Class is not the only factor. Weight, distance, origin and destination, accessorial services such as liftgate or residential delivery, and the carrier's own tariff all play a part. But class is the one you control most directly through how you pack.

## The 2025 changes

NMFTA's Docket 2025-1 took effect on [July 19, 2025](https://nmfta.org/news/nmfta-implements-historic-nmfc-changes-launches-classit-to-support-industry-in-adapting-to-new-classification-standards/). It was one of the largest overhauls of the NMFC in years. The goal, in NMFTA's words, was to simplify the system, eliminate redundancies and improve clarity around packaging requirements and density-based assignments.

What that means for shippers:

- **One standard density scale.** Many commodity-specific classes were replaced with a single 13-tier density scale that applies when there are no special handling, stowability or liability concerns.
- **More classification by density.** More freight with similar characteristics now shares the same class.
- **Lower classes for heavier freight.** NMFTA lists lower density classes for heavier freight among the expected effects for shippers.
- **Packaging matters more.** Because density is calculated on the shipment as tendered, the way you pack directly changes your class.

Not every item uses the standard scale. Freight with handling, stowability or liability concerns can still have its own class or its own density scale. If you are unsure, look up your item's NMFC number or ask your carrier.

## The 13-tier density scale

Here is the standard scale from NMFTA's [Disposition Bulletin for Docket 2025-1](https://nmfta.org/wp-content/media/2025/03/Disposition-Bulletin-1371-1.pdf):

| Sub | Density (pounds per cubic foot) | Class |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Less than 1 | 400 |
| 2 | 1 but less than 2 | 300 |
| 3 | 2 but less than 4 | 250 |
| 4 | 4 but less than 6 | 175 |
| 5 | 6 but less than 8 | 125 |
| 6 | 8 but less than 10 | 100 |
| 7 | 10 but less than 12 | 92.5 |
| 8 | 12 but less than 15 | 85 |
| 9 | 15 but less than 22.5 | 70 |
| 10 | 22.5 but less than 30 | 65 |
| 11 | 30 but less than 35 | 60 |
| 12 | 35 but less than 50 | 55 |
| 13 | 50 or greater | 50 |

## How to measure your shipment

Density only works if the measurements are right. NMFTA's guidance comes down to a few rules.

### Use extreme dimensions

NMFTA says the NMFC requires using the shipment's extreme dimensions, meaning the greatest straight-line length, width and height, including all packaging such as pallets, crates or do-not-stack cones. If a box overhangs the pallet, measure to the edge of the box, not the pallet. If the top is uneven, measure to the highest point.

### Include the pallet

Height is measured from the floor to the top of the load, including the pallet. Weight includes the pallet, packaging and stretch wrap. NMFTA's [density guidance](https://nmfta.org/news/decoding-density-the-freight-factor-you-cant-afford-to-overlook/) names forgetting the pallet or packaging height as a common mistake, along with rounding measurements too generously.

### Measure each handling unit

A handling unit is the piece the carrier handles as one unit, such as a pallet, crate or skid. If your shipment has several handling units, measure each one.

### Measuring checklist

- [ ] Measure length, width and height in inches, down to the inch
- [ ] Measure to the furthest point of any overhang
- [ ] Include the pallet height
- [ ] Measure to the highest point of an uneven top
- [ ] Weigh the complete handling unit, pallet and wrap included
- [ ] Record each handling unit separately

## How to calculate density

Once you have dimensions and weight, the math has three steps.

1. **Find the cubic inches.** Multiply length × width × height, in inches.
2. **Convert to cubic feet.** Divide cubic inches by 1,728, the number of cubic inches in one cubic foot.
3. **Find the density.** Divide the weight in pounds by the cubic feet.

### Worked example from NMFTA

NMFTA's FAQ on the 2025 changes uses this example: a pallet measuring 48 × 40 × 45 inches and weighing 450 pounds.

- 48 × 40 × 45 = 86,400 cubic inches
- 86,400 ÷ 1,728 = 50 cubic feet
- 450 ÷ 50 = 9.00 pounds per cubic foot

On the standard scale, 9 pounds per cubic foot falls in Sub 6 (8 but less than 10), which is class 100.

### What happens when the pallet gets taller

Say you stack the same goods on a taller, looser pallet, 48 × 40 × 60 inches, with the same 450 pounds.

- 48 × 40 × 60 = 115,200 cubic inches
- 115,200 ÷ 1,728 = about 66.7 cubic feet
- 450 ÷ 66.7 = about 6.75 pounds per cubic foot

That drops into Sub 5 (6 but less than 8), class 125. The goods are the same. Only the packing changed, and so did the class.

## How to keep your class down

- **Pack tighter.** Fill empty space and avoid oversized boxes.
- **Avoid overhang.** Freight that hangs past the pallet edge adds to the measured footprint.
- **Choose the right pallet.** An oversized pallet adds cube without adding product.
- **Build stackable loads.** A flat, stable top makes freight easier to stow, while do-not-stack cones count toward the measured height.
- **Measure before you book.** Use the same numbers on the bill of lading that the carrier will find if it re-measures.

## What happens if the class is wrong

LTL carriers can inspect, re-weigh and re-measure freight. If your shipment does not match the class on the bill of lading, the carrier can reclassify it and bill you at the correct rate, often with an added fee. NMFTA notes that accurate classification is how shippers avoid billing surprises and reclassification charges.

The best protection is accurate dimensions, a recorded weight and, for regular shipments, the correct NMFC item number for your product.

## Bottom line

Freight class turns your shipment's density, handling, stowability and liability into a rating that LTL carriers use to price it. Since July 19, 2025, a standard 13-tier density scale covers much of the NMFC, so measuring carefully matters more than ever. Measure extreme dimensions, include the pallet, divide weight by cubic feet, and check the result against the scale before you book.
