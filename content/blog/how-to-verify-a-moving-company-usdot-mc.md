Hiring an interstate mover means handing a stranger everything you own and trusting them to show up at the other end. The good news is that the federal government keeps a public record on every registered interstate mover, and checking it takes about ten minutes.

This guide walks through the lookup step by step, explains what the key fields mean, and lists the warning signs that should make you stop.

## Why the numbers matter

Interstate movers must be registered with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) and have a USDOT number. That is the baseline. But registration is not the same thing as permission to move your household goods.

FMCSA spells this out on its [registered vs. authorized movers page](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/consumer-protection/protect-your-move/are-fmcsa-registered-moving-companies-same-fmcsa-authorized): "A company may be registered but not authorized to move household goods." To move your belongings across state lines for pay, a carrier also needs the right operating authority, which is identified by a docket number with an MC prefix.

| Identifier | What it is | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| USDOT number | The company's federal registration ID | Who they are, where they are based, inspection and crash history |
| MC number | Docket number for interstate operating authority | Whether they are allowed to haul for hire across state lines, and what type of cargo |
| Operating authority status | The current state of that authority | Authorized, not authorized, or out of service |

## Step 1: Get the numbers in writing

Ask the company for its legal name, USDOT number and MC number before you book. A legitimate interstate mover will give them without hesitation, and FMCSA requires brokers to show their MC number in their advertising.

Write down the exact legal name. Many moving companies operate under a "doing business as" (DBA) name that differs from the name on the federal record. You need both to match what you find.

## Step 2: Search the FMCSA mover database

FMCSA runs a [search tool built for consumers](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/search-mover). According to FMCSA, the results show the company's headquarters location, contact information, registration status, the type of business (carrier, broker or freight forwarder), complaint information and safety information.

Search by USDOT number first. If you search by name, you may see several companies with similar names, which is one reason scammers pick generic ones.

## Step 3: Open the SAFER Company Snapshot

For a more detailed look, enter the USDOT or MC number in the [SAFER Company Snapshot](https://safer.fmcsa.dot.gov/CompanySnapshot.aspx). This is the record FMCSA publishes for every registered company. Here are the fields worth reading closely.

### Entity Type

This says whether the company is a carrier, a broker, a shipper or some combination. If you think you are hiring a moving company with trucks and crews but the record says broker only, you are talking to a middleman.

### USDOT Status

You want to see **Active**. An inactive USDOT number can mean the company did not complete its required biennial update, which is a sign the business may not be operating normally.

### Operating Authority Status

This is the most important line. The SAFER help page lists the values you may see:

- **Authorized for HHG** means the company holds authority to transport household goods. This is what you want for an interstate household move.
- **Authorized for Property** means it can haul general freight, but that alone does not cover household goods.
- **Not Authorized** means the company has an active USDOT number but no operating authority.
- **Out-of-Service** means the carrier is under an out-of-service order and is not allowed to operate.

A mover whose snapshot shows an active USDOT number but "Not Authorized" for operating authority should not be moving your belongings across state lines.

### MC/MX/FF Number

This is where the docket number appears. Check that it matches the MC number the company gave you. If the field is blank, or it shows a different number, ask why.

### Carrier Operation and Cargo Carried

Carrier Operation should include interstate if you are crossing state lines. Cargo Carried should include household goods. A company whose only listed operation is "Intrastate Only" is not set up for an interstate move.

### Inspections, crashes and safety rating

The snapshot shows inspection and crash counts for the past 24 months. A brand-new company may have none, which is not by itself a problem. A long list of out-of-service inspections is.

## Step 4: Confirm insurance is on file

FMCSA lists minimum [insurance filing requirements](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/insurance-filing-requirements) for each type of authority. A motor carrier of household goods must file both public liability coverage and cargo insurance. Brokers file a surety bond or trust fund instead, because they never take possession of your goods.

From the Company Snapshot, follow the licensing and insurance link to see the filings on record. You are looking for current liability and cargo filings under the same company name. If the insurance has been cancelled or there is nothing on file, the authority can be revoked, and you would have little recourse if something went wrong.

Insurance on file is not the same as coverage for the full value of your belongings. That is a separate topic, covered in our guide to released value vs. full value protection.

## A note on FMCSA's new registration system

FMCSA has been moving registration into a new system called Motus. Its May 2026 notice says new registrations receive randomized USDOT and docket numbers to prevent fraud, and some new USDOT numbers carry a letter suffix showing the entity type, such as "B" for broker or "C" for property carrier. Existing numbers stay the same, and MC, MX and FF docket prefixes continue as before.

FMCSA has also [proposed](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/motus-usdot-number-suffixes) making the USDOT number the sole identifier and no longer assigning MC numbers, but says that change requires rulemaking and will not take effect with the first release of the system. For now, check both numbers.

## Step 5: Check complaints and reviews

FMCSA's mover search includes complaint information. FMCSA also recommends checking the Better Business Bureau and your local consumer protection office. Read the complaints, not just the count. A pattern of the same problem, such as higher prices at delivery or goods held until extra payment, matters more than a single angry review.

## Red flags during verification

Stop and ask questions if you notice any of these:

- [ ] The company will not give you a USDOT or MC number, or gives you one that belongs to a different company name
- [ ] The snapshot shows "Not Authorized" or "Out-of-Service" for operating authority
- [ ] Operating authority covers property but not household goods
- [ ] Carrier Operation is intrastate only, but they are quoting an interstate move
- [ ] The record lists the company as a broker, but they say they have their own trucks and crews
- [ ] No insurance is on file, or it has been cancelled
- [ ] The address on the record is a residence or mail drop that does not match the address they gave you
- [ ] The company name is generic and many look-alike companies appear in search results

FMCSA keeps a broader list of [moving fraud red flags](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/red-flags), including demands for large deposits and estimates given sight unseen. We cover those in our article on moving scams and how escrow payment protects you.

## Movers vs. brokers

Plenty of legitimate moves are booked through brokers. FMCSA's movers vs. brokers guidance explains the difference: a mover owns trucks and assumes responsibility for your goods, while a broker arranges the move and is not authorized to transport goods itself. Registered brokers must give you a list of the movers they use and work only with registered movers.

If you book through a broker, verify the carrier that will actually show up, not just the broker.
