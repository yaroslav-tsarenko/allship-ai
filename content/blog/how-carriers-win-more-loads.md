On a load board where carriers bid, every load is a small competition. The shipper sees several offers side by side and picks one. Price matters, but it is rarely the only thing that decides it.

This guide covers what makes a bid stand out: picking the right loads, responding at the right time, writing notes shippers can act on, setting pickup windows you can keep, and building a record that wins the next load for you.

## Start with the loads you can actually run well

The fastest way to lose money on a load board is to bid on everything. The fastest way to win loads is to bid where you have an edge.

### Know your lanes

A lane is a route you run regularly, such as a city pair or a region you know. Bidding on your lanes helps in three ways:

- **Better pricing.** You know your real costs on that route, including tolls, fuel, parking and the deadhead back.
- **Better timing.** You can offer pickup dates that line up with trucks you already have nearby.
- **Fewer surprises.** You know the access issues, the weigh stations and the traffic patterns.

Think about the load that gets you home or to your next pickup, not just the load in front of you. A slightly lower bid on a load that eliminates an empty return can earn more than a higher bid that leaves you deadheading for hours.

### Match equipment honestly

If a shipper is moving a four-bedroom house, they need the right truck, the right crew and the right blankets and straps. If they are moving a skid steer, they need a trailer and tie-down setup rated for it. Bid only when your equipment fits the load as described, and say so in your notes.

### Filter before you scroll

Set your service area, equipment types and the load types you take, and filter the board by them. You will spend less time skipping irrelevant loads and more time writing good bids on the right ones.

## Respond fast, but not carelessly

Shippers tend to compare bids while the load is fresh in their minds. Getting a solid bid in early puts you in front of them during that window.

But speed is only useful if the bid is right. Before you submit, check:

- [ ] Pickup and delivery locations, including access notes like stairs, long carries or no truck parking
- [ ] Item list, weight, dimensions or volume
- [ ] Requested dates and how flexible the shipper is
- [ ] Special handling, such as non-running vehicles, fragile items or oversized freight
- [ ] Whether your equipment and crew fit the job

A fast bid you have to revise later, or a load you win and then cannot run, hurts more than a bid that arrived an hour later.

## Write bid notes a shipper can act on

The bid note is where you turn a number into a decision. Most shippers do not know trucking jargon. They want to know what they get, when, and what could change the price.

### What strong bid notes include

- **What is included.** Loading, unloading, blanket wrap, disassembly, straps, liftgate, or whatever applies to the load.
- **Equipment.** "26-foot box truck with liftgate and two-person crew" says much more than "truck available."
- **Pickup window.** A specific date and time range.
- **Transit time.** When the shipper can expect delivery.
- **Assumptions.** What your price is based on, such as the item list provided, ground-floor access or a running vehicle.
- **What changes the price.** Be upfront about anything that would add cost, so the shipper is not surprised on site.

### A weak note vs. a strong note

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Can do. Good price. Call me." | "Two-person crew, 26 ft box truck with liftgate. Pickup Tue 8–11 am, delivery Thu by 5 pm. Price includes blanket wrap and bed disassembly and reassembly. Based on the item list posted and elevator access at both ends." |
| "Open carrier available." | "Open car hauler. Pickup window Mon–Tue, delivery 4–5 days after pickup. Car must run and steer; if not, winch fee applies as stated in chat." |

The strong notes answer the shipper's next three questions before they ask. That is what makes a shipper comfortable choosing you.

### Keep the tone professional

Write in full sentences, skip the all caps and avoid pressure tactics. A shipper handing over their household goods or a high-value machine is looking for the carrier who sounds calm and organized.

## Set pickup windows you can actually keep

Shippers plan their lives around your pickup window. They take time off work, schedule elevator reservations or line up a forklift. Missing the window is the quickest way to get a bad review.

Build your window from your real schedule, not your best case. Account for loading time at the previous stop, traffic, and federal hours-of-service limits. FMCSA's [hours-of-service summary](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/regulations/hours-service/summary-hours-service-regulations) sets, for property-carrying drivers, a maximum of 11 hours driving after 10 consecutive hours off duty, no driving beyond the 14th consecutive hour after coming on duty, and a 30-minute break after 8 cumulative hours of driving. A pickup window that only works if the driver ignores those limits is not a real window.

If something changes, tell the shipper early. A message the day before saying you will be two hours late is far better than a no-show.

## Keep your documentation ready

Shippers increasingly check carriers before they book, and platforms check them before they can bid at all. Keeping your paperwork current saves you from losing loads to an expired document.

### What to keep current

- **USDOT registration.** Shippers can look you up in the [SAFER Company Snapshot](https://safer.fmcsa.dot.gov/CompanySnapshot.aspx). Make sure your legal name, address and operation details are current.
- **Operating authority.** For-hire carriers hauling regulated freight across state lines generally need operating authority. FMCSA's [types of operating authority](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/types-operating-authority) separates motor carriers of property from motor carriers of household goods, so make sure your authority matches the loads you bid on.
- **Insurance.** FMCSA's [insurance filing requirements](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/insurance-filing-requirements) list the minimum public liability coverage by authority type, plus cargo insurance for household goods carriers. Keep your certificate of insurance current and in the same legal name as your authority.
- **Load paperwork.** Bills of lading, inventories, inspection reports and delivery receipts. Clean paperwork protects you if a shipper disputes a delivery.

### Photos protect you

Take photos at pickup and at delivery: the condition of the goods, how they were loaded and secured, and the condition at drop-off. If a dispute comes up, photos with timestamps settle most questions quickly.

## Reviews from completed loads

Every load you complete is a chance to win the next one. Shippers comparing bids look at reviews, and reviews from real completed loads carry weight because they show what happened, not what was promised.

How to earn good ones:

- Show up inside the window you promised
- Communicate during the trip, especially if anything changes
- Handle the goods the way your notes said you would
- Walk the shipper through delivery and any paperwork
- Fix small problems on the spot rather than arguing about them

One honest, specific review from a completed job is worth more than any claim in a bid note.

## Bottom line

Carriers who win more loads on a bidding board are not always the cheapest. They bid on lanes they know, respond quickly with accurate numbers, write notes that answer the shipper's questions, commit to pickup windows they can keep, keep their paperwork current and earn reviews one completed load at a time.
